How thoughtful exhibition design connects geology, evolution, and local ecosystems.

Shenzhen is widely known for electronics manufacturing, hardware development, and technology companies. But the city has also been developing major public science and cultural facilities.

I recently visited the Shenzhen Natural History Museum in Pingshan District. The museum has enormous dinosaur skeletons, mineral collections, fossils, animal specimens, and immersive video installations.

What impressed me most, however, was not the scale of the museum.

It was how the exhibitions connect individual scientific facts into a coherent story.[https://medium.com/media/7941385dedefa92b178f3e7c2cf9c112/href](https://medium.com/media/7941385dedefa92b178f3e7c2cf9c112/href)From Plate Tectonics to Rocks and MineralsThe geology exhibition begins with the structure of the Earth and the processes that shape its surface.

Visitors then encounter igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks, followed by minerals and large crystals.

A beautiful crystal can attract attention on its own. But after learning about geological processes, visitors can also consider how and under what conditions it formed.

The museum connects something as large as the movement of the Earth’s crust with a single rock in front of the visitor.

[Watch the geology exhibition (05:04)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uEnwdK1DB6E&t=304s)From Natural History to EvolutionThe evolution section introduces the history of natural history: how people observed, collected, described, and classified living organisms.

From there, the exhibition moves toward Darwin’s theory of evolution and natural selection.

Fossils then become more than unusual objects. They provide evidence for understanding the history of life, evolutionary change, and extinction.

This sequence helps visitors understand not only scientific facts, but also how scientific explanations developed.

[Watch the evolution exhibition (06:51)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uEnwdK1DB6E&t=411s)

From Individual Animals to EcosystemsThe museum also has extensive collections of animal specimens.

But after presenting individual species, the exhibition shifts toward ecosystems, introducing mountains, oceans, grasslands, and other natural environments.

The perspective changes from identifying organisms to understanding their relationships with other organisms and their surroundings.

[Watch the ecosystem exhibition (15:33)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uEnwdK1DB6E&t=933s)Bringing the Story Back to ShenzhenThe exhibition begins with the universe and the history of the Earth. It moves through evolution, dinosaurs, and ecosystems around the world.

Near the end, it returns to the natural environments of Shenzhen itself, including Shenzhen Bay, the Shenzhen River, and local wildlife.

This was one of my favorite parts of the museum.

After exploring billions of years of natural history, visitors encounter the ecosystems surrounding the city where they live.

[Watch Shenzhen’s local ecosystems (17:18)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uEnwdK1DB6E&t=1038s)Good Curation MattersNatural history museums need important collections and scientific specimens. But collecting objects and communicating their meaning are different tasks.

The Shenzhen Natural History Museum demonstrates how exhibition design can connect established scientific knowledge through physical objects, spatial arrangements, and the sequence in which visitors encounter them.

I previously visited the Shenzhen Science and Technology Museum in Guangming District, which connects scientific principles with computing, robotics, and industrial applications.

The two museums have different subjects, but both show how the organization of scientific knowledge can shape visitors’ understanding.
