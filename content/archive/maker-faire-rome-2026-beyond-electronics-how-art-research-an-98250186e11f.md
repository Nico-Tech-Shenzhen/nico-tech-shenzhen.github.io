Maker Faire Rome 2026: Beyond Electronics — How Art, Research, and Industry Come Together in Europe’s Maker CultureNico-Tech Shenzhen Field Notes | Masakazu Takasu

I attended Maker Faire Rome 2026, held on October 23–25 at Gazometro Ostiense in Rome, Italy. This was my second year participating as part of the M5Stack exhibition team.

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*d9lTdF-DKYiLTnFw8JJkVQ.png)

With more than 400 exhibition stands, Maker Faire Rome is one of Europe’s major maker events. But what makes it interesting is not simply its size.

Unlike maker events that focus primarily on electronics, 3D printing, and robotics, Maker Faire Rome brings together university research, environmental technology, industrial design, interactive art, education, established companies, and individual makers.

I recorded a 47-minute walking tour of the exhibition, visiting as many areas as possible.

[Watch the full Maker Faire Rome 2026 field report on YouTube — English version available](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE)

Rather than describing every exhibit, I want to use selected scenes from the video to explain what makes Rome different from Maker Faire Tokyo, Maker Faire Shenzhen, and other events I have visited around the world.

Each timestamp in this article links directly to the corresponding scene.Last year, I wrote that Rome was bringing technology into its art and design cultureDuring autumn 2025, I visited six Maker Faires across Seoul, Tokyo, Rome, Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Taipei.

I wrote about how each city’s Maker Faire reflects its local industries, educational institutions, and maker communities.

[Read my comparison of six Maker Faires in 2025 (Japanese)](https://note.com/takasu/n/nac0d9f9cc0b5)

My observation of Rome was that its DIY culture had traditionally been strong in art and design, while electronics and advanced technology were less prominent than in places such as Tokyo or Shenzhen.

At Maker Faire Rome 2025, however, universities, research institutions, government organizations, and technology companies had become increasingly involved. Their participation brought more robotics, computer science, electronics, and research demonstrations into the event.

The result was not simply a larger exhibition. It was a more diverse one.

In 2026, I felt this trend was even more visible.

According to the Rome Chamber of Commerce, this year’s event included 26 secondary schools, 27 universities and research institutions, and 145 projects from those universities and research institutions.

That is a significant academic presence for a maker event.

And the researchers were not separated from the rest of the fair. Their projects appeared alongside DIY vehicles, student games, environmental technology, art installations, and commercial products.

I think this combination is one of Rome’s greatest strengths.A former industrial gasworks becomes a city-scale innovation exhibitionThe venue itself tells an interesting story.

Maker Faire Rome takes place at Gazometro Ostiense, a former gasworks site dominated by an enormous steel gas-holder structure.

[Watch 06:49 — The extraordinary Gazometro venue](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=409)

The old industrial buildings, open spaces, and large steel structures have become exhibition halls and public spaces.

It is a very different atmosphere from a conventional convention center.

The event is organized by the Rome Chamber of Commerce, with substantial involvement from public institutions and industrial partners. Even public buses and metro stations around Rome carry Maker Faire advertisements.

[Watch 00:24 — Maker Faire promotion around Rome](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=24)

The exhibition is organized around three themes: Innovation, Creativity, and Discovery.

[Watch 01:10 — The three exhibition themes](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=70)

These are not just marketing labels. The themes bring together different groups of participants.

For example, the Lazio regional government’s area includes FabLab projects, startup support, and demonstrations connecting local businesses with digital fabrication and biotechnology.

[Watch 03:16 — Lazio’s FabLab and startup ecosystem](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=196)

Government involvement in a maker event can sometimes result in a very conventional industrial exhibition.

But Rome seems to use public support to bring more people into the maker ecosystem, rather than replacing individual makers with corporate displays.

There are school projects, experimental works, and unusual DIY creations throughout the venue.

The public sector provides the infrastructure, while a wide range of participants bring their own projects.

That distinction matters.University research belongs at a Maker FaireOne of the most impressive parts of Maker Faire Rome is the number of academic research demonstrations.

Walking into one area, I found underwater drones, DIY rockets, CubeSats, lunar rovers, and various projects built by university students.

[Watch 04:29 — University research and underwater drones](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=269)

[Watch 05:04 — An open-source CubeSat project](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=304)

[Watch 05:28 — Student-built lunar rovers](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=328)

Many of these projects could easily appear at a university open day or an engineering research exhibition.

But displaying them at Maker Faire changes the audience.

University researchers are no longer presenting only to fellow researchers or industry specialists. Children, artists, hobbyists, product developers, and families encounter their projects directly.

This creates opportunities for conversations that would not happen at an academic conference.

Later in the tour, I found research involving virtual reality, haptic feedback, interfaces, and assistive technology.

[Watch 37:30 — Haptics and VR research next to games](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2250)

[Watch 39:28 — Engineering research presented to a broader audience](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2368)

I participate in academic research on developer-centric hardware platforms, so this was particularly interesting to me.

Conferences such as CHI and other HCI research events feature many impressive interactive demonstrations. Some of these projects could attract considerable public interest if presented at Maker Faire.

Academic papers are essential for research communication, but they are not the only way research creates value.

Sometimes, putting a working prototype in front of someone from a completely different field is just as important.

The researcher learns how outsiders perceive the technology, while visitors discover possibilities they did not know existed.

Maker Faire Rome provides a particularly good environment for that exchange.Italian design culture meets digital fabricationNot everything at Maker Faire Rome is about electronic circuits.

One project was P3DALO’, a roughly five-meter-long pedal boat made using desktop 3D printing.

[Watch 07:14 — P3DALO’, the 3D-printed pedal boat](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=434)

It is designed to be disassembled for transportation, demonstrating how digital fabrication can produce large, functional objects.

Nearby was Cyberpandino, a cyberpunk-style customized Fiat Panda.

[Watch 08:19 — Cyberpandino, a completely transformed Fiat Panda](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=499)

There were also large sculptures, custom vehicles, robotic musicians, and numerous works combining traditional materials with modern fabrication methods.

[Watch 08:54 — Robots playing music](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=534)

In Tokyo, the technical sophistication of individual DIY projects is often extraordinary. In Rome, I see a different emphasis: digital fabrication as an extension of art, industrial design, furniture making, and creative craftsmanship.

The two approaches are not opposed, but their starting points can be different.

For many Italian makers, technology appears to be a tool for realizing a design or artistic concept, rather than the central subject of the project.

This contributes to a very different variety of exhibits.Sustainability, biology, food science, and DIYAnother distinctive feature of Rome is how many projects involve environmental technology, biology, and materials.

For example, I encountered an automated mushroom cultivation system using IoT sensors to monitor growing conditions.

[Watch 12:29 — IoT-based mushroom cultivation](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=749)

Nearby, another project used AI and computer vision for automatic waste sorting.

[Watch 12:51 — AI-powered waste sorting](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=771)

There was also research on converting used coffee grounds and other food waste into useful materials.

[Watch 13:01 — Turning food waste into new resources](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=781)

And, of course, Precious Plastic and other projects transforming discarded plastics into panels, furniture, and accessories.

[Watch 13:47 — Precious Plastic and material upcycling](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=827)

These exhibits are interesting because they do not necessarily begin with a particular microcontroller or fabrication tool.

They begin with a problem: wasted materials, environmental monitoring, agriculture, or production processes.

Digital fabrication, sensors, AI, and open hardware become tools for addressing those problems.

The exhibition also included fermentation technology startups, energy-monitoring systems, and DIY food preparation machines.

[Watch 16:29 — Yeastime and fermentation technology](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=989)

[Watch 18:34 — DIY energy monitoring](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=1114)

[Watch 19:39 — An automatic cocktail maker and cooking robots](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=1179)

I think this is an important direction for maker culture.

The value of making is not limited to producing electronic gadgets.

Affordable sensors, fabrication tools, and open-source software allow people from many different disciplines to develop working solutions.

In Rome, the combination of technology, material science, design, and environmental awareness was particularly visible.When an energy company creates an interactive art exhibitionOne of the most memorable areas was an immersive exhibition by Eni, the Italian energy company.

It was located in a darkened space beneath the industrial structures, with large displays, light installations, video art, and interactive experiences.

[Watch 23:33 — Entering the immersive exhibition space](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=1413)

[Watch 24:17 — Eni’s “Pioneers to Makers” installation](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=1457)

The installation, called Pioneers to Makers, commemorated the centenary of Agip, founded in 1926.

Instead of presenting a conventional corporate-history exhibition, it used archive materials, games, digital imagery, and interactive artwork.

I found the approach quite interesting.

An established energy company could have presented its industrial history through panels, videos, and technical explanations.

Instead, it collaborated with creative professionals to turn that history into an experience.

This is another example of how Maker Faire Rome connects groups that do not usually share the same exhibition environment.

Large industrial companies, young designers, digital artists, and technology developers are all part of the event.

In some respects, the curation of these relationships is more interesting than any single installation.Arduino’s home country — and an increasingly international hardware ecosystemOf course, electronics and robotics are also major parts of Maker Faire Rome.

Arduino, STMicroelectronics, DigiKey, Microchip, Raspberry Pi, M5Stack, and several other hardware companies had a substantial presence.

[Watch 27:12 — Entering the Electronics & IoT area](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=1632)

At the STMicroelectronics booth, I found an OpenArm demonstration.

[Watch 27:34 — OpenArm at the STMicroelectronics booth](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=1654)

I have been involved in supporting the OpenArm ecosystem through Enactic, so seeing the open-source robot arm demonstrated at an international hardware exhibition was especially encouraging.

OpenArm is designed as an open-source dual-arm robotic platform for embodied AI and robotics research.

An important part of such platforms is allowing developers to work with capable, accessible hardware instead of building every mechanical and electronic component from scratch.

The demonstration combined OpenArm with the Arduino VENTUNO Q platform.

Arduino itself had a large exhibition area.

[Watch 29:09 — Arduino’s robotics demonstrations and VENTUNO Q](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=1749)

VENTUNO Q combines a Qualcomm Dragonwing IQ8 processor (QCS8275) for AI applications with an STM32 microcontroller for real-time control.

[Watch 30:00 — The Qualcomm processor inside VENTUNO Q](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=1800)

It illustrates how far Arduino has evolved from its early image as a simple microcontroller platform for beginners.

Today, the ecosystem extends into Linux, edge AI, computer vision, and advanced robotics.

I also had a brief encounter with Arduino co-founder Massimo Banzi.

[Watch 30:46 — Meeting Arduino co-founder Massimo Banzi](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=1846)

Arduino began in Italy and became a global open-hardware ecosystem. Seeing its founders, developers, users, and industrial partners gathering at Maker Faire Rome shows how closely the maker movement and hardware industry have developed together.

A maker platform helps people build projects. Some of those developers create products or companies. Those companies then contribute tools, knowledge, and support back to the community.

That cycle is an essential part of the maker ecosystem.M5Stack in Italy: From product awareness to a local communityI participated in Maker Faire Rome as part of the M5Stack team.

M5Stack is a Shenzhen-based hardware company offering modular development tools used for electronics, IoT, education, and prototyping.

This was our second year exhibiting in Rome.

[Watch 31:37 — The M5Stack booth and its visitors](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=1897)

Compared with last year, I felt that far more visitors already knew M5Stack products.

Instead of simply asking what M5Stack was, people came with specific questions about products and applications.

A Stack-chan robot tracking faces with AI attracted attention, and the booth was busy throughout the event.

[Watch 32:00 — Stack-chan and M5Stack demonstrations](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=1920)

One of the most interesting developments was the participation of Antonio, a local Italian M5Stack enthusiast who helped explain products to visitors.

[Watch 34:02 — Antonio helping at the M5Stack booth](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2042)

For many Italian visitors, especially children, English is not necessarily the easiest language for technical conversations.

Having a local user explain products in Italian made a considerable difference.

There is an important distinction between a manufacturer introducing its products and an existing user demonstrating why those products are useful.

The second creates a different kind of trust.

Products such as the Cardputer were already familiar to many visitors, and several items sold out during the event.

[Watch 34:30 — Growing familiarity with M5Stack products](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2070)

[Watch 34:44 — Products selling out at the booth](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2084)

This experience connects directly with my academic research on developer-centric hardware platforms.

Hardware ecosystems do not grow only through advertising or conventional sales channels.

Developers build projects, share their work, recommend products, create tutorials, and bring in other developers. Some of those projects eventually lead to commercial adoption.

Local communities play an important role in making this process sustainable.

An international exhibition becomes much more valuable when it helps create relationships that continue after the event ends.Humanoid robot soccer, interactive art, and HCI research — all in one placeThe diversity continued in the robotics and gaming areas.

Booster Robotics demonstrated humanoid robots playing soccer.

[Watch 35:06 — Booster Robotics and humanoid robot soccer](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2106)

Only a short distance away, Rome Art Week presented interactive media installations.

[Watch 35:38 — Interactive art from Rome Art Week](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2138)

Further inside, there were games, VR experiences, haptic interfaces, educational technology, and academic research projects.

[Watch 38:15 — Games and advanced VR research under one roof](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2295)

There were also areas dedicated to digital craftsmanship, 3D-printed design objects, and experimental fabrication.

[Watch 40:24 — Digital Artisan and product design](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2424)

For younger visitors, Maker Camp offered games and hands-on activities.

[Watch 42:28 — Maker Camp and educational games](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2548)

[Watch 44:43 — Electronics workshops using ESP32-C6](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2683)

Individually, many of these exhibits could belong to completely different events: an art exhibition, a robotics conference, an engineering open house, or a children’s science festival.

At Maker Faire Rome, they share the same space.

That is the real strength of the event’s curation.

People who come for games encounter academic research. Designers encounter robotics. Engineers encounter art and biotechnology.

These encounters do not guarantee new collaborations, but they create possibilities that would otherwise be difficult to arrange.

I think this is one of the most valuable functions of a Maker Faire.Comparing Tokyo, Shenzhen, and RomeHaving attended Maker Faires in several cities, I have become increasingly interested in how differently they develop.

I recently wrote a field report on Maker Faire Tokyo 2026.

[Maker Faire Tokyo 2026 — Field report (Japanese)](https://note.com/takasu/n/n4478d2a3fb9a)

Tokyo remains one of the best Maker Faires in the world in terms of the technical quality, originality, and diversity of individual DIY projects.

It is remarkable how many highly sophisticated projects are built by individuals or small groups without the intention of immediately turning them into commercial products.

Shenzhen is different.

Its maker ecosystem is closely connected to electronics manufacturing, hardware startups, supply chains, and increasingly AI and robotics companies.

Projects can move rapidly between prototyping and commercial development.

Rome has a different strength.

It connects the region’s design and art culture with universities, public institutions, established industries, technology companies, and individual makers.

[Watch 40:03 — Comparing Tokyo’s DIY projects with Rome’s approach](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2403)

Public institutions and large companies can sometimes make maker events feel too commercial or bureaucratic.

But at Rome, student projects, experimental artworks, small DIY creations, and major industrial demonstrations still coexist.

This balance is difficult to achieve.

And it is not simply a matter of increasing exhibition space.

It requires bringing together communities with different motivations, resources, and expectations.Maker Faire as part of a regional innovation ecosystemWhen people discuss Maker Faires, they often focus on the most impressive individual projects.

Those projects are important, of course. But the value of an event also depends on the relationships it creates.

Maker Faire Tokyo demonstrates the extraordinary creative and technical capabilities of individual makers.

Maker Faire Shenzhen connects DIY culture with hardware manufacturing and startups.

Maker Faire Rome shows how art, research, industry, education, and public institutions can be brought into the same maker ecosystem.

[Watch 46:01 — Final observations from Maker Faire Rome 2026](https://youtu.be/14rogXe2buE?t=2761)

For me, Maker Faire Rome is a good example of how a maker event can become part of a city’s wider innovation ecosystem without losing its DIY character.

Not every project needs to become a startup. Not every maker needs to be an engineer. Not every research prototype needs an immediate commercial application.

What matters is creating a place where people with different backgrounds can encounter one another’s work.

After visiting Rome for the second year, I feel that the event is getting better at doing exactly that.

M5Stack also received a much stronger response than last year, and I hope we can participate again.

For anyone interested in maker culture beyond electronics, Maker Faire Rome is well worth visiting.
